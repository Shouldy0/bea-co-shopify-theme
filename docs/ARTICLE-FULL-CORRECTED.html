Signs of Separation Anxiety in Dogs: What Most Owners Miss

Not every bark is anxiety. Not every chewed shoe is boredom. But when the patterns start to repeat, it's worth noticing.

If you've found this post, chances are you're wondering whether what your dog is experiencing might be more than just "missing you." Maybe you've come home to scratched doors, concerned neighbors, or a dog who acts like you've been gone for years when it was only twenty minutes.

You're not alone in this. And you're not imagining things.

What separation anxiety actually looks like

Separation anxiety isn't one behavior — it's a pattern. And recognizing the pattern starts with noticing the small signals.

The most common signs include:

  • Vocalization that starts shortly after you leave — barking, whining, or howling that begins within minutes of departure (not hours later)
  • Destruction focused on exit points — scratched doors, chewed window frames, damaged door handles
  • House soiling despite being housetrained — accidents that only happen when your dog is alone
  • Pacing or inability to settle — following you room-to-room when home, panicking when you move toward the door
  • Refusal to eat — ignoring treats or food in your absence, even high-value rewards

The key distinction: these behaviors happen specifically in connection with your departure or anticipated departure. A dog who chews shoes at 3pm while you're at work is different from a dog who chews the doorframe within 10 minutes of you leaving.

What most owners miss

Here's what often gets overlooked: the anticipation phase.

Many owners tell us they didn't realize their dog was anxious because the "obvious" signs only happened when they were gone. But the buildup often starts much earlier.

Watch for these pre-departure signals:

  • Following you unusually closely when you're getting ready to leave
  • Showing signs of stress (panting, pacing, drooling) when you pick up keys or put on shoes
  • Trying to physically block your exit
  • Becoming withdrawn or refusing interaction

These are your dog's way of saying "I don't know what's happening next, and that's uncomfortable."

The guilt trap (and how to step out of it)

If you're reading this, there's a good chance you're also carrying some guilt. Maybe you've thought:

  • "I shouldn't have gotten a dog if I have to work."
  • "Other dogs are fine alone — what am I doing wrong?"
  • "Maybe I'm just being dramatic."

Here's what we want you to know: separation anxiety is not a reflection of your love or your competence. It's a real behavioral condition, thought to affect around one in five dogs — and noticing it, really noticing it, is the first step toward supporting your dog through it.

You're not being dramatic. You're paying attention.

When to seek professional support

Separation anxiety exists on a spectrum. Mild cases often respond well to structured observation and gradual desensitization. But there are times when professional guidance is important:

  • If your dog is injuring themselves (broken teeth, cut paws from escape attempts)
  • If the anxiety is severe enough that your dog cannot be left alone at all
  • If you've tried consistent approaches without improvement
  • If your dog shows signs of panic (excessive drooling, self-harm, complete refusal to eat)

Noticing is the first step

You don't need to have all the answers today. You don't need to fix everything overnight.

But if you've recognized your dog in any of these signs, you've already done something important: you've started paying attention.

And sometimes, that's exactly where the path forward begins.
